The Basics: What Even is Carbon Fiber?

If you were like me and have only heard of carbon fiber as a cure-all near-indestructible material, you might be wondering what it actually is made of. At its core, its simply a weave of carbon fibers (as the name suggests). The strong part comes from the fact that its often mixed with resin and hardener and given time to cure in a tuned environment. After this process, you're left with an incredibly stiff material, while maintaining a surprisingly low weight. This obviously has a wide variety of applications, especially in the field of automotives. On our electric motorcycle, we primarily use it as the primary material for our fairings. The fairings are the external components that make up the classic aerodynamic shape of a motorcycle.

Designing a Gas Tank (Kind Of?)

As I mentioned before, my team was tasked with designing the gas tank... which doesn't store any gas. It's just for electrical components, which leads me to one of the first design challenges. This part serves two main purposes: provided a base for the rider to rest their chest on and to protect the electronic components. One of the interesting characteristic of carbon fiber is that it's incredibly conductive - which is not a good property to have when it will be in close contact with electronics. This is pretty easily solved by using a single layer of fiberglass on the inside of the part. So with that problem solved, we can focus entirely on designing an ergonomic part! We started by using Solidworks 2024 to come up with a preliminary design.

Redesign, redesign, and redesign

Our first design for the gas tank was inspired by what we'd seen on other AHRMA motorcycles. However, simple Solidworks FEA revealed that it was pretty weak, especially on the large flat surface. I would like to note here that our simulations were purely relative - we hadn't found a good way to emulate the unique properties of carbon fiber in an FEA environment as it is anisotropic (and depends heavily on curing). This is something our team plans on elaborating on in the coming years; we're currently looking into RAVEN for composites simulation.

We also quickly discovered that the gas tank simply wasn't large enough. There were a lot of electronics going underneath this casing, which meant the gas tank would have to be equally as large. This led to our final redesign. The key features were cut out leg bracing on the sides, a larger flat surface towards the rider to rest their chest on, and of course, infinite space. We also ran a Solidworks fluid simulation on it, but honestly it was mostly for show. We didn't have any benchmark values we wanted to meet and the UBC wind tunnel is still not operational. We also plan on moving to ANSYS to do more useful simulations that will actually guide our decision making.

Manufacturing: A Learning Process

We had finally reached the most arduous part of the process: actually manufacturing the gas tank. The whole proccess is split into two sections: making the mo(u)ld (depending on where you're from) and the wet-layup, both coming with their own unique challenges. Unfortunately for us, there was not much documentation to guide us through this - last years' projects were practically lost to time. We had to figure it out as we went.

For the molds (I'm just going to drop the 'u'), we used stacked layers of stiff insulation foam. To shape the layers, we used the CNC Router available to us in the Engineering Design Center. This led us to our first challlenge - creating the G-Code for the CNC. This is normally not too difficult, but the original pathing algorithim we used was slow. Like, very slow. We used adaptive clearing for nearly everything, which meant that each layer took more than an hour. Each mold had over 10 layers, which meant 10 hours of sitting in front of the CNC machine. This was brutally painful.
