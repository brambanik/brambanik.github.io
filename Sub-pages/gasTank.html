Carbon Fiber Gas Tank

September 3, 2025

I initially applied for a position on UBC Thunderbikes on a whim. All of my friends sung the praises of being on a design team, and I felt like it would be a good avenue to develop skills for my resume. After the interview, I was subsequently placed on the aerodynamics team. I thought I would be in for many weeks of training before even touching the bike, but instead I was immediately assigned to work on the gas tank (which is a bit of a misnomer, it's really just a compartment to hold to electronics). I will admit I was overwhelmed with the responsibility, but it turned out to be an incredibly formative experience in terms of improving my understanding of composites manufacturing.

The Basics: What Even is Carbon Fiber?

If you were like me and have only heard of carbon fiber as a cure-all near-indestructible material, you might be wondering what it actually is made of. At its core, its simply a weave of carbon fibers (as the name suggests). The strong part comes from the fact that its often mixed with resin and hardener and given time to cure in a tuned environment. After this process, you're left with an incredibly stiff material, while maintaining a surprisingly low weight. This obviously has a wide variety of applications, especially in the field of automotives. On our electric motorcycle, we primarily use it as the primary material for our fairings. The fairings are the external components that make up the classic aerodynamic shape of a motorcycle.

Designing a Gas Tank (Kind Of?)

As I mentioned before, my team was tasked with designing the gas tank... which doesn't store any gas. It's just for electrical components, which leads me to one of the first design challenges. This part serves two main purposes: provided a base for the rider to rest their chest on and to protect the electronic components. One of the interesting characteristic of carbon fiber is that it's incredibly conductive - which is not a good property to have when it will be in close contact with electronics. This is pretty easily solved by using a single layer of fiberglass on the inside of the part. So with that problem solved, we can focus entirely on designing an ergonomic part! We started by using Solidworks 2024 to come up with a preliminary design.

Redesign, redesign, and redesign

Our first design for the gas tank was inspired by what we'd seen on other AHRMA motorcycles. However, simple Solidworks FEA revealed that it was pretty weak, especially on the large flat surface. I would like to note here that our simulations were purely relative - we hadn't found a good way to emulate the unique properties of carbon fiber in an FEA environment as it is anisotropic (and depends heavily on curing). This is something our team plans on elaborating on in the coming years; we're currently looking into RAVEN for composites simulation.

We also quickly discovered that the gas tank simply wasn't large enough. There were a lot of electronics going underneath this casing, which meant the gas tank would have to be equally as large. This led to our final redesign. The key features were cut out leg bracing on the sides, a larger flat surface towards the rider to rest their chest on, and of course, infinite space. We also ran a Solidworks fluid simulation on it, but honestly it was mostly for show. We didn't have any benchmark values we wanted to meet and the UBC wind tunnel is still not operational. We also plan on moving to ANSYS to do more useful simulations that will actually guide our decision making.

Manufacturing: A Learning Process

We had finally reached the most arduous part of the process: actually manufacturing the gas tank. The whole proccess is split into two sections: making the mo(u)ld (depending on where you're from) and the wet-layup, both coming with their own unique challenges. Unfortunately for us, there was not much documentation to guide us through this - last years' projects were practically lost to time. We had to figure it out as we went.

For the molds (I'm just going to drop the 'u'), we used stacked layers of stiff insulation foam. To shape the layers, we used the CNC Router available to us in the Engineering Design Center. This led us to our first challlenge - creating the G-Code for the CNC. This is normally not too difficult, but the original pathing algorithim we used was slow. Like, very slow. We used adaptive clearing for nearly everything, which meant that each layer took more than an hour. Each mold had over 10 layers, which meant 10 hours of sitting in front of the CNC machine. This was brutally painful.

It got to the point where we couldn't stand the time it took that we looked into alternatives. We quickly came across the speedy parallel cut. This worked incredibly well but it had another issue - it was so fast that the drill bit would head up beyond what the cooling fluid could cool. Subsequently, the excess cut foam would melt on to the drill bit, making it unusable. I wish I still had an image to show of this. We came up with a temporary solution: literally just vacuum the foam before it could melt on the drill bit. As scuffed as it is, it worked. After putting the mold together, along with filling gaps from poor manufacturing, we arrived at the last step of the process.

As a side note, to avoid running into these mundane issues in future years with new recruits, I've started creating training documents. You can find the one for generating toolpaths here!

The Final Step

Lastly, and most importantly, we moved on the the wet layup. You may be wondering: Why not use vacuum infusion for this part? It provides more consistent resin distribution and limits imperfections! The simple answer to that is we weren't very experienced. Unfortunately, I wasn't available to particpate in this layup (I had class, how studious of me), but my wonderful teammates did a pretty good job for a first time!

Although the layup looked clean and dare I say phenomenal, we ran into some issues during curing. The most obvious was the ridges that developed when we put the part under vacuum - small air gaps and not completely flat fibers led to creases forming. These solids creases were unappealing and impossible to remove without damaging the part. We also had a very difficult time taking the part off the mold, which we can only assume was because not enough mold release was applied. Both of these led to the unprofessional finish you see in the image at the beginning of this article. However, we were really happy with how much we learned from these mistakes and also the fact that we had succesfully designed, CAD'd, and manufactured a part out of carbon fiber!

Moving Forward: MotoStudent Bound

When we the gas tank, we were met with some sad news. AHRMA, the competition we were planning to go to, had suddenly decided to stop supporting electric motorcycles for racing. This meant we were out of competition and, to an extent, out of a purpose. Luckily, we got one soon enough. Almost immediately after, MotoStudent contacted us, proposing that we enter their competition. MotoStudent is the premier leagues, so we were enthralled to be given the opportunity. Thus begins our two-year design cycle for the next competition, set to be in Spain!

From an administrative standpoint, I was promoted to a Co-Lead position, meaning I was now in charge of the new recruits. With what I had learned from the previous year in mind, I set some clear goals for this year: better documentation, cleaner manufacturing, and incorporating physical tests in our design process. We also decided a big list of new projects that we'll be beginning with our new team:

  • Front Fairing
  • Side Fairings
  • Tail Fairing
  • Gas Tank
  • Mud Guards
  • Miscellaneous Forged Fiber Parts
  • Rear Frame (yes, the whole rear frame)

I'm particularly looking forward to reinforcing our old manufacturing methods and trying out some new ones, like forged carbon fiber for more complicated parts! And with that, I've wrapped up most of my first year at UBC Thunderbikes. I can't wait to see what we'll achieve in the next one!
